Building a Conveyor Frame with SOLIDWORKS Weldments

One sketch, one multibody part, and a cut list the workshop can order from

Table of Contents

Key takeaways

  • Drive the frame from one fully defined skeleton sketch.
  • Group structural members so trims and corners follow the design.
  • Keep welded steel in the part and bolted equipment in the assembly.

A conveyor frame is mostly repetition: two long stringers, a row of cross members, legs, and a head and tail arrangement at either end. Modelled as separate extrusions, every length change becomes a hunt through the feature tree. Modelled as a weldment, the frame is driven by one skeleton sketch, and the cut list comes out of the model rather than being typed by hand.

Start with the skeleton, not the steel

Open a part, start a 3D sketch, and draw the frame as centre lines only: the belt running line, the head and tail pulley axes, the frame width, and the leg positions. Nothing here is steel yet. Every dimension you will be asked to change later — length, width, height, incline angle — should live in this sketch, and ideally in a handful of named global variables such as "Frame_Length" and "Belt_Width".

With the skeleton fully defined, Insert > Weldments > Structural Member turns those lines into steel. Select a profile, then pick sketch segments in groups. Keep each group to segments that share a corner treatment and an alignment: the two stringers in one group, the cross members in another, the legs in a third. Groups are what make the corners behave.

Name the groups as you go

Rename each structural member feature the moment you create it — "Stringers_100x50", "Crossmembers_50x50" — rather than leaving Structural Member1, 2, 3. On a frame with six or seven member types, that habit is the difference between a model a colleague can pick up and one they rebuild from scratch.

Corner treatment and trims

Inside a group, SOLIDWORKS offers End Miter, End Butt1 and End Butt2. Miters look tidy on show frames; butt joints are usually cheaper and quicker for a conveyor that will be hidden under guarding. Between groups, use Trim/Extend to cut members back against a face or another body. The setting to watch is "Weld gap" — a small, deliberate gap (commonly 1–2 mm, but confirm it with the fabricator) leaves room for the weld and stops bodies from sharing faces.

  • Trim cross members to the inner faces of the stringers, not to a sketch line, so they follow if the width changes.
  • Add gussets at the leg-to-stringer joints with the Gusset feature rather than modelling plates by hand.
  • Cap open tube ends with End Cap, which also keeps water and debris out of the real frame.
  • Use Fillet Bead for welds you want on the drawing; skip cosmetic beads everywhere else, as they add rebuild time and clutter.

Why one part, and when to stop

A weldment is a multibody part, and that is exactly right for anything that arrives at site as one welded item. It is the wrong tool for the whole conveyor. Keep bolted and bought-in items — drive unit, pulleys, bearings, guarding — as separate parts in an assembly, and bring the weldment in as one component. A useful rule: if it is cut and welded together in the shop, it belongs in the weldment; if it is bolted on afterwards, it belongs in the assembly.

The payoff arrives at the first revision. Change Frame_Length in the skeleton sketch and the stringers grow, the cross members redistribute if they were patterned, the gussets follow their joints, and the cut list renumbers itself. Nothing is retyped, so nothing is missed.
