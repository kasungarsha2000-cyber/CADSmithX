Assembly Mates That Survive a Design Change

Width, profile centre and slot mates, and why mating to faces catches you out

Table of Contents

  • The mates worth reaching for
  • Keeping the moving parts moving

Working on something similar?

Have a mechanism that needs to move correctly? Share the travel, interfaces, and any parts already fixed in the design.

Key takeaways

  • Choose mates that describe the intended relationship.
  • Reference stable planes and axes where possible.
  • Check motion and interference across the full travel.

Three coincident mates and a distance will hold almost anything still, which is why so many assemblies are built that way. They also tie the component to three specific faces, and faces move. Better mates say what the designer means: this bracket sits centred on that channel, this pin runs in that slot.

The mates worth reaching for

MateUse it whenWhat it saves you
WidthCentring a part between two facesStays centred when the gap changes
Profile CenterLanding a plate or boss on a rectangular or circular faceOne mate instead of three
SlotA bolt or pin that must travel in a slotKeeps travel honest, with limits
Limit distance / angleDoors, covers, take-up travelShows the real range of movement
LockSub-assemblies that never move relative to each otherFewer mates, faster rebuilds

Where you can, mate to planes, axes and the origin rather than faces. Reference geometry does not vanish when a fillet is added or a face is split, so the mate holds. For bought-in parts that are inserted again and again — bearings, actuators, castors — add a Mate Reference to the part once, and every future insertion snaps into place correctly.

Read the mate errors from the top

When an assembly turns red, resist the urge to delete mates from the bottom of the list. The first error is usually the real one, and the rest are consequences. Fix the top error, rebuild, and watch most of the others disappear.

Keeping the moving parts moving

A sub-assembly is rigid by default, so a gripper inserted into a machine will not open. Set it to flexible when you need to show it working, and back to rigid when you do not — rigid rebuilds faster, and on a large machine that difference is felt all day. For a mechanism you want to demonstrate at fixed positions, Mate Controller stores those positions and steps between them without you dragging anything.

Then test it properly. Drag the mechanism through its full travel with collision detection on, and run Interference Detection across the whole assembly. Both take a minute, and both find the kind of clash that is expensive once it exists in steel.
